Hank’s Tank · a machine-learning curriculum built from your own results

Ten machine-learning lessons your MLB project already taught

Every concept below is a standard machine-learning idea. Every piece of evidence for it comes from your own models and your own data, not from a textbook example. You built a demonstration of the central problems in applied ML, largely by accident.

Lesson 01

Some problems have almost no learnable signal, and knowing that is a skill

The concept — irreducible error

Every prediction problem has a floor: the part of the outcome that is genuinely random. Model quality is not measured against perfection, it is measured against that floor and against the best achievable. In most tutorials the signal is large and the floor is far away. In yours, the floor is almost the whole thing.

Accuracy hides this. The home team wins ~53% of games for free, so “53% accurate” means zero information. The honest unit is nats of log-loss improvement over that free baseline. Measured on one consistent sample:

The consequence

When total available signal is 0.019 nats, the difference between a good model and a great one is a rounding error. The difference between an honest evaluation and a sloppy one is 7 percentage points (see Lesson 3). In low-signal domains, evaluation discipline matters more than model sophistication, the opposite of the usual instinct.

Lesson 02

The bias-variance tradeoff, drawn by ten versions of your own model

The concept

Adding features reduces bias (the model can express more) but increases variance (each parameter must be estimated from finite data, and estimation error is noise the model then trusts). Total error is the sum. Past some point, more features make predictions worse. Textbooks draw this as a U-curve; you generated one.

The decisive test: an automated search was allowed to pick

450 Optuna trials searched 7 model families over 174 features, scored by walk-forward log-loss with feature selection inside each fold. It converged on L1 logistic regression over 3 features, and spent 265 of 300 trials there because it kept winning.

Ranked by feature strategy: 3 hand-picked features 0.68871, top-k by in-fold AUC 0.68985, all 161 features 0.69315. More features was monotonically worse.

Lesson 03

Leakage is how ML projects lie to themselves, and yours did it five ways

The concept — data leakage

Leakage is when information reaches the model that would not exist at prediction time. It does not look like a bug; it looks like success. Every instance below produced a number someone believed. These are the five archetypes, and you now have a real example of each.

The two questions that catch all five

Ask of every training column: what was the timestamp on this value, and would I have had it before first pitch? That one question catches all five. The V8 team-ID failure needs a second question: is this feature an identity or a measurement? Identities memorise; measurements generalise.

Lesson 04

How you split your data decides what you learn

The concept — validation protocol

A random train/test split assumes rows are exchangeable. Time-series rows are not: a random split lets the model train on August and test on May, learning from the future. Walk-forward (expanding window, always predicting forward) is the only honest protocol here, and it costs you accuracy on paper.

Your V7 generation discovered this the hard way

V7’s holdout said 55.5%. Walk-forward cross-validation on the same model said 53.8%. The 1.7-point gap was pure protocol. Your own docs draw the right conclusion: “holdout accuracy overstates true generalisation by 1.5–2%.”

Today’s work added a stricter rule: a candidate must win in two independent windows. On this data, a single-window improvement has been wrong every time it was checked: the margin models, the ensembles, the historical-transfer models and the Elo “edge” all looked good in one window and died in the other.

The trap I walked into today

If you tune anything — a threshold, a blend weight, a feature set — on data you then report results from, you have leaked. Today the blend weight was first set at 0.5 by hand and scored P(better)=0.988. Refitting that same weight inside each training fold dropped it to P=0.876. The apparent significance was me having chosen the number while looking at the answer.

Lesson 05

Accuracy is usually the wrong metric, and your simulator proves it

The concept — proper scoring rules

Accuracy throws away the probability and keeps only which side of 0.5 it fell on. Near 0.5 that is nearly pure noise. Log-loss and Brier score the probability itself and are proper: they are optimised by telling the truth. AUC scores ranking only, ignoring calibration entirely. They can disagree, and when they disagree the disagreement is the finding.

Lesson 06

Calibration is a separate property from accuracy, and it is where your product lives

The concept

A model is calibrated if, among games it calls 62%, about 62% happen. Calibration and discrimination are independent: a model can rank perfectly and still print nonsense numbers. Since your product surfaces confidence tiers, calibration is the product.

Read your own fix carefully

That shrinkage also improved accuracy by +1.11pp, which looked like a free win. Inspecting which predictions changed showed all 164 flipped picks were away→home, in the narrow band hp ∈ [0.42, 0.50). The trivial rule “pick home unless hp < 0.48” scored identically.

So it was never a calibration fix — it was a home-field bias correction wearing a calibration costume. Always ask what a change actually did, not just whether the metric moved.

Lesson 07

Statistical power: how much data a claim needs before you are allowed to make it

The concept

Every measured number is an estimate with an interval around it. Small samples produce wide intervals, so a small sample can show a huge effect that is entirely noise. Compute the sample size a claim requires before looking at results.

Multiple comparisons — the reason 67 rules found six “winners”

Testing many hypotheses guarantees false positives. At α = .05, 67 candidate rules manufacture ~3.4 significant results before any real effect exists. The defence is to discover on one window and confirm on another, and to count and report how many things you tried.

Lesson 08

A worked example of a false positive being found and killed

The concept — adversarial verification

When a result you want appears, the correct response is to attack it. Not to check it once more the same way — to attack it from angles that would each independently expose an artifact.

The habit to keep

A single-split p-value on this data has been misleading every time it was checked. Treat P > 0.95 from one split as a hypothesis, not a result, and require: full-period evaluation, walk-forward, a held-out season, and an economic test. If it survives all four, believe it.

Lesson 09

More features is not more information

The concept

A feature helps only if it carries signal that the model does not already have, in enough quantity to pay for the parameter it costs. Those are three separate conditions, and today produced a clean example of each failing on its own.

The strongest form of this test: does your model know anything the market doesn’t?

Fit logit(market_prob) alone, then logit(market) + logit(your_model), training on one period and testing on another. If your features carry information the market lacks, the second model wins.

Result: −0.00065 nats, P(helps) = 0.050. Coefficients: market +0.3906, every one of yours ≤ 0.041 with several wrong-signed. Betting the disagreements returned −10.0% ROI. Your features are a lossy restatement of what the line already knows. It is also why weather and injuries both measured null: they are public, so they are already priced.

Lesson 10

Advanced structures, and what each one actually taught

The concept

Beyond fitting a classifier there are model structures: simulate the generating process, track a latent state over time, shrink estimates toward a prior, verify one implementation against another. Each was built today, and each taught more than its accuracy score did.

The takeaway

Ten rules, earned rather than read

Each of these exists because something in this project went wrong without it. They transfer to any supervised-learning problem you touch next.

    What is hard to learn any other way

    You now know what a low-signal problem feels like from the inside: where ten generations of feature engineering produce less than one evaluation bug, where the simplest model wins, and where the honest answer to “can we beat the market” is no, with receipts. Most people never get a clean measurement of that. It makes you much harder to fool on the next problem, including by yourself.
